MS Warrior: Tony Harris’ Story The open road began to close in on Tony Harris as he drove his truck up Interstate 95 toward Baltimore in 2008. The street signs and pavement appeared as if he were looking at them through a sheer, grey curtain.

As the day progressed, things looked darker still. By the time he arrived at his destination, patches of his vision were blocked altogether. "Losing my vision was terrifying," Tony recalls. "I was too shocked to understand what exactly was happening to me.

I've never experienced anything like that before." Tony called his sister and they went directly to the hospital. His hands and feet were numb, and as his legs gave out, he realized he was experiencing one of the worst flare-ups of Multiple Sclerosis (MS) he can remember. "It felt like the floor was moving independently of my feet," he says "...and my body wasn't listening to my brain." About Multiple Sclerosis (MS) Multiple sclerosis is an unpredictable, often disabling disease of the central nervous system that disrupts the flow of information within the brain, and between the brain and body. The cause of MS is still unknown - scientists believe the disease is triggered by as-yet-unidentified environmental factor(s) in a person who is genetically predisposed to respond.

Learn more about MS and options for managing symptoms, treatments, coordination of health care, and living well with MS. Source: http://www.nationalmssociety.org MS: A Heavy Weight The energetic father of four had been making a good living as a truck driver when MS stole his livelihood. After his first experiences with fatigue and numbness, Tony thought he could "power through." But when the strange symptoms returned, bringing vertigo and loss of vision, he knew it was time to hand in his keys. A neurologist explained that Tony needed to take some time to get his health in order, take proper medication and give his body the rest it demanded.

In 2009, he began receiving Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) while he sought treatment. He was grateful that the vision loss was temporary, and that he would have an opportunity to focus on managing his condition. But Tony also had concerns about providing for his children. "It was depressing," he recalls.

"I was in a dump physically and mentally. MS does a number on your emotional state. I was paid a decent salary, and all of that was gone. I couldn't stop thinking, 'Wow, how can we survive off this [disability check]?

What am I going to do?'" Heavy Weight Champion Tony was determined to regain his health and strength, but had not considered that his efforts would also lead to a new career path. He read about how to manage MS, and learned that exercise could be an effective means of minimizing relapses and flare-ups. So Tony hit the gym. A little bit at a time, he focused on strengthening individual muscle groups.

He worked on different muscles every day and fell in love with being at the gym. "I saw an immediate, positive effect," he recalls. "I started challenging myself - at first slowly - and before I knew it, I needed to move to a different gym, where the weights were heavier!" "I had two kids in college and considered the example I could set. Life gets hard for everyone at some point.

No one's life is as easy as you think it is. But that doesn't mean you should quit." By the end of 2011, Tony had recovered much of his well-being and felt he was ready to return to the workforce. He went to Maryland's Department of Rehabilitative Services (DORS), a state vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency, where he received career counseling. Tony decided he would look into becoming a fitness instructor, and DORS offered assistance as he pursued a new career.

"I wasn't certified as a fitness instructor," he recalls. "So I went to the Community College of Baltimore County to earn my certification and DORS paid for it!" After finishing school, Tony found work at LA Fitness. DORS closed his case early in 2012. LA Fitness offered valuable work experience, but his new employer made cuts and Tony soon found himself looking for work again.

By the end of the year, he landed some part-time work at a gym called Fitness 19 in Parkville, MD. Tony was glad to have found employment, but he was not yet able to pay his bills. As he added to his experience, Tony decided it was time to explore full-time employment options. Ticket to Work and Work Incentives When picking up his SSDI check, Tony learned from a Social Security field office employee about the Ticket to Work program.

Ticket to Work supports career development for people with disabilities who are ready for employment. Adults age 18 through 64 who receive Social Security disability benefits qualify. Through the Ticket to Work program, service providers known as Employment Networks (ENs) offer a range of free support services to help people prepare for, find, or maintain employment. Some ENs serve people with a specific type of disability; others offer certain types of services.

The program is voluntary and set up to help people progress toward financial independence. An employee at a Social Security field office connected Tony with an EN in Baltimore called Full Circle Employment Solutions, and in 2013, his pursuit of a full-time career gained momentum. The first thing he needed was help understanding the risks and rewards connected with full-time employment. With help from a benefits counselor at Full Circle, Tony learned about Social Security rules called "Work Incentives." Work Incentives make it easier for adults with disabilities to explore work and still receive health care and some cash benefits from Social Security.

Tony was able to test his ability to work during a nine month "Trial Work Period" (TWP), while still receiving cash benefits. After his TWP ended, a 36-month "Extended Period of Eligibility" (EPE) began. During the EPE, most people with a disabling impairment get benefits for months in which they earn less than $1,090 (Social Security's definition of "Substantial Gainful Activity"). Tony felt at ease knowing he was not going to suddenly lose his health care coverage and SSDI benefits when he returned to work.

He would have time to determine how full-time employment would work for him. With help from Full Circle, Tony developed an Individual Work Plan, and set goals that would help him expand his role at Fitness 19. Continuation of Healthcare Coverage for People with Disabilities Who Work Several Work Incentives allow SSI and SSDI recipients who work to continue receiving healthcare coverage, even after they no longer receive cash benefits. Continuation of Medicare Coverage for SSDI Beneficiaries: Most people with disabilities who work will continue to receive at least 93 consecutive months of Hospital Insurance (Part A); Supplemental Medical Insurance (Part B), if enrolled; and Prescription Drug coverage (Part D), if enrolled, after the 9-month Trial Work Period (TWP).

You do not pay a premium for Part A. Although cash benefits may cease due to work, you have the assurance of continued health insurance. (93 months is 7 years and 9 months.).To qualify, you must have a disability, already have Medicare and be working at SGA. Medicare for Persons with Disabilities Who Work (SSDI only): If your Medicare stopped due to work, you continue to have a disabling impairment, and you are under age 65, you can buy continued Medicare coverage.

If you have limited resources, you may be eligible for state assistance under various Medicare Savings Programs. Your state Health and Human Services agency makes the determination about whether you qualify for this help. Medicaid While Working (Section 1619b) for SSI Beneficiaries: After you return to work, your Medicaid coverage can continue, even if your earnings become too high to receive SSI payments. To qualify, you must meet all eligibility rules (including the resources test), need Medicaid in order to work, and have gross earned income that is insufficient to replace SSI, Medicaid and any publicly funded attendant care.

For more detailed information about these Work Incentives, read the Redbook at www.ssa.gov/redbook, or call the Ticket to Work Help Line to find a provider that can help you. For help, you can also call the Ticket to Work Help Line at 1-866-968-7842 (V) or 1-866-833- 2967 (TTY). Ticket to Work Service Providers Several different types of Ticket to Work providers can help you transition to the workforce or progress in the job you have: Benefits counselors (also known as benefits advisors) are professionals who can explain how working will affect your Social Security disability benefits. Community-based organizations known as Work Incentives Planning and Assistance Projects (WIPA), have benefits counselors on staff.

Some authorized Ticket to Work providers, known as Employment Networks (ENs), also offer benefits counseling services. 600+ Employment Networks across the US offer a range of free support services through Ticket to Work. Some ENs serve specific populations, while others may provide specialized support services. ENs can help you: Prepare for the workforce Find a job and stay employed Advance in your current job Get job accommodations Stay in touch with Social Security Stay Organized Workforce ENs are providers that are also part of a state's public workforce system.

Like other ENs, Workforce ENs can give you access to a wide array of employment support services, including training programs and special programs for youth in transition and veterans. A Ticket to Work participant who assigns their Ticket to a Workforce EN will work with providers in the workforce system (including American Job Centers). People who need more intensive support services (such as rehabilitation or training) may find help at a State Vocational Rehabilitation (VR) agency. VR agencies furnish a wide variety of services to help people with disabilities go to work.

These services are designed to provide the client with the training and other services that are needed to return to work, to enter a new line of work, or to enter the workforce for the first time. VR can help you get ready to work, and, if necessary, you can then find an EN who can help you keep your job and make more money. Use the "Find Help" tool at www.choosework.net to connect with providers who offer the services "The services I received through the Ticket program at Full Circle helped me find what I was looking for," he recalls. "They were very supportive.

I began with benefits counseling and then we discussed career options. I knew I wanted to work in fitness and they helped me better understand the fitness market. I also received support services after I found work! I meet with staff at Full Circle every month.

They help me stay organized, report my earnings, and communicate with Social Security." The MS Warrior Tony's Trial Work Period began in July of 2012, when he found work as a personal trainer at Fitness 19. He has been helping people improve their health and fitness ever since. With higher earnings, he left SSDI behind and says it is nice to no longer rely on a fixed income. He is developing a new workout video as part of a program geared to helping other people with MS regain their balance, agility and strength.

Tony has attacked this new project with the same drive he deployed in his fight against the disease that threatened his livelihood. "Returning to work has given me hope. Being employed gives you the money you need to do the things you have to do. I didn't have hope when I was on disability waiting for a check to come.

That's not who I am and it's no way to live. Now I'm inspired by how physical fitness can change lives. It's clear each time I look at my clients' before-and-after pictures!" Ticket to Work and Work Incentives helped Tony find his path to a better future. Find yours!
