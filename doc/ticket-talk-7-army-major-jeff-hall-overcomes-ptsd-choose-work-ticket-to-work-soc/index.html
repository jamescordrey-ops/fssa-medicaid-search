July 2013 This Ticket Talk podcast episode focuses on PTSD, an anxiety disorder that can develop after extreme emotional trauma. In this interview we spoke with Army Major Jeff Hall, a combat veteran, who served in Baghdad and is a spokesperson for the Department of Defense’s Real Warriors Campaign. Download audio file (mp3) Download transcript Transcript Opening: You are listening to the Social Security's Ticket to Work podcast series.

Get answers to your questions, access information and resources, and receive expert advice on Work Incentives and the Ticket to Work program. Interviewer: Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder or “PTSD” as it’s commonly known, is an anxiety disorder that can develop after extreme emotional trauma. It’s normal to feel sad, anxious, or frightened after a traumatic event, but for people experiencing PTSD, the symptoms may be disruptive to daily life and persist for months or even years. The disorder has received increased attention in recent years, with up to 20 percent of returning combat warriors of the Iraq and Afghanistan wars experiencing symptoms.

In addition to combat, many catastrophic events can trigger PTSD, such as terrorist attacks, abuse, violent crime, or natural disasters. Like depression and other anxiety disorders, PTSD is “invisible,” which can make it difficult to recognize. To bring awareness to this disorder, we’re speaking with Army Major Jeff Hall, a combat veteran and spokesperson for the Department of Defense’s Real Warriors Campaign. Major Hall experienced PTSD after a traumatic encounter with an improvised explosive device (IED), during one of his deployments to Iraq.

The Real Warriors Campaign is a public awareness campaign designed to encourage service members, veterans and military families coping with these invisible wounds to seek assistance. Major Hall, thank you for joining us. Major Hall: Well, thank you for asking. Interviewer: Will you please describe your job during your second deployment to Iraq?

Hall: I was the commander of Alpha Battery, Fourth Battalion, First Artillery out of Fort Riley, Kansas. We were stationed north of Baghdad in Taji, and I owned land around the camp for security reasons. Interviewer: So, you were the security perimeter there at the camp? Hall: Yes, I actually owned land.

We did a lot of counter insurgency operations and combat operations north of Taji. Interviewer: Will you describe how you felt emotionally and mentally after returning from your second deployment? Hall: I felt um emotionally empty, as if I had completely ran out of gas um emotionally. And mentally, I had become completely numb to um everything that I was wanting to do or desired to do when I came home.

I just couldn’t seem to put the war down. So, I was pretty much exhausted. Interviewer: So it really came home with you? Hall: Yeah, I drug it home every time I came home.

Interviewer: And, what affect did that emotional trauma have on your family? Hall: In effect, it pretty much tore my family apart as we knew it. Uh, I isolated or alienated myself away from my family. And of course, they were wondering what was wrong with Jeff and or dad.

And um, there were no answers for that because I tried to keep the war from them the best I could. But, I just couldn’t take my mind off of it. Therefore, it kind of pushed my family away. Interviewer: Now, how did those experiences impact your job?

Hall: I pretty much become non-mission-capable at my job. And what I mean by that was um I had things I wanted to train, there were experiences I knew on the battlefield. And then we have this thing called doctrine that we also try to train to. And uh, I became conflicted with it.

I also had some moral wounds that that came along with combat too that kept me conflicted in my day-to-day business. And it got to the point where I could not conduct myself professionally anymore. My anger would become visceral and I had been known to put my hands on people to get them to do what I wanted them to do, which was not a good thing to do in the position I was in. Interviewer: So, it really got to the point where you could not work?

You could not function in your normal routine in your career? Hall: That is correct. As a matter of fact, the day that it really came home, I found myself unable to leave my house. Uh, and I don’t know what the barrier was, I just couldn’t do it.

I couldn’t go back to work. And that is when the command kind of picked up the ball and ensured that I got to see some help or seek help. Interviewer: Is that when you first recognized that you were experiencing PTSD? Hall: Actually, you know in retrospect, I should have recognized that I had some PTSD after my first deployment when I came back.

I was reacting to things. I was driving widely around trash that was on the highway. There was actually [a] car that blew a tire next to me when I was driving my family to the city of Manhattan, Kansas. And I reacted to that as if I had been in an ambush, which would be turn into it.

And, I should have recognized there was something wrong then, but I kind of just laughed it off. But, uh, I really realized it, I’d say the day that the curtain came down and I wasn’t able to go to work. I just, I just couldn’t function anymore. Interviewer: So when did you seek help?

Hall: I actually sought help around April of 2008 and the first couple of encounters were not good for me, because I kind of kept everything close guarded. I didn’t really want to share my experiences with anybody. But, uh my command got involved and sent me to Walter Reed and at Walter Reed, at the deployment health clinical center, that’s when I actually got help. Interviewer: And what kind of treatment did you receive at that time?

Hall: I received a uh, it was actually a program of individual care, class work and group therapy and physical therapy. Where they literally looked at us from the top of our heads to the tip of our toes to figure out if there was something physically happening that might be causing the issues. And I found that I did have a cracked spine, and it was causing me a lot of pain issues which kind of uh exacerbated the entire situation. And they started to deal with some of my pain.

And, uh my wife was able to come with me to Walter Reed. And I was able to, well; I was actually made to listen to her side of the storyof her side of the deployment. And then, she listened to my side that I had been keeping away from her and with that, we kind of were able to piece our relationship back together. Interviewer: Major Hall, how important is it to have family with you as part of the treatment process for PTSD?

Hall: I think it’s extremely important and the reason why [is that] it’s an invisible wound. And it’s hard to look at somebody who you know and be able to see the actual wound itself. And with them being able to be there with you in the treatment when the psychologist is able to pull out the things that are really affecting you and let them see it, it really helps in understanding. I mean, some of it needs to stay private.

But it definitely helps for [your family] to be there with you so that you’re not having to explain. They kind of understand the treatment too. And, not only that, PTSD is contagious, I really believe that. And there can be a secondary effect for the people that are around you when you’re not acting yourself.

You can really, uh affect the people around you and I believe that has happened in my own family too. So, it’s important that you go through it together. Interviewer: Wow, is treatment an ongoing thing? Is it something that you still work on?

Hall: It is something [that is] ongoing. I still see a professional. But, I would say, 80 percent of my healing is self-aid and not in the form of alcohol, like some people choose to do. My self-aid is through artwork and finding something meaningful to give back.

And I’ve kind of become a peacenik if you will, in a way. But it just feels good to me and that’s helped me more than anything else. Interviewer: That’s wonderful. So what advice would you offer someone who thinks he or she may have PTSD?

Hall: Well, the best advice I could give is to not hide it. Uh, I don’t believe there’s any…there’s no excuse for bad behavior just because you have PTSD. However, you may not recognize your bad behavior when you’re doing it. Um, the best thing you can do is talk.

Talk to your family. Talk to friends. Talk to people who might have like experiences and reach out to other veterans that from other wars or whatever. I have found it incredible, reaching out to other veterans who have experienced combat and finding that we are very similar in the way we think about a lot of things.

And, it’s been comforting to me and it’s also helped them too. So, I would recommend that they talk to other veterans. Talk to their families. Don’t hide anything.

Don’t think that um your experiences are so terrible that you can’t share that burden with people that love you. Interviewer: That sounds like most excellent advice from someone who’s been through it. Major Hall, we really thank you for speaking with us today. We appreciate you sharing your experience and knowledge about PTSD with our listeners.

Hall: Well, thank you for asking me. Interviewer: According to Social Security, PTSD, like many mental illnesses, can be classified as a disability. If fear, sadness and anxiety following a traumatic event are disruptive to your daily life and your ability to work for a period of 12 months or longer, it’s important to seek help.
