FAQ

Medicaid

How do I apply for Indiana Medicaid?

You can apply online through the FSSA Benefits Portal, in person at a local Division of Family Resources (DFR) office, by mail, or by phone at 1-800-403-0864. A certified navigator can help you apply for free.

Indiana Medicaid for Members: Apply for Coverage (in.gov) Open original page

Who can get Medicaid in Indiana?

Medicaid covers several groups, including children, pregnant individuals, parents and caretakers, adults ages 19 to 64, and people who are 65 or older, blind, or disabled. Each group has its own rules and income limits, and a caseworker runs the full rules on your situation.

Indiana Medicaid for Members: Eligibility Guide (in.gov) Open original page

How long does a Medicaid application take?

After you submit a complete application, it can take up to 90 days to get a decision. You can check your application status online or by calling 1-800-403-0864 with your case number. Pregnant applicants may be able to get care while they wait through presumptive eligibility.

Indiana Medicaid for Members: Apply for Coverage (in.gov) Open original page

What if I disagree with a Medicaid decision?

You have the right to appeal, and it costs nothing to file. For a decision about your eligibility, follow the appeal process in the notice the Division of Family Resources sent you. Appeals about health care actions must be filed within 33 calendar days of the decision.

Indiana Medicaid for Members: Member Appeals (in.gov); IHCP Member Eligibility and Benefit Coverage module (PROMOD00009) Open original page

Do I have to report changes after I am approved for Medicaid?

Yes. Report any change in your circumstances that could affect your eligibility to the Division of Family Resources within 10 days of the change, or of when you learned about it.

IHCPPM Chapter 2000: Application Processing Open original page

What is Medicaid estate recovery?

When a Medicaid member dies, Indiana is required by federal and state law to try to recover what Medicaid paid from the member's estate. The recovery is handled by the Medicaid Estate Recovery Program at FSSA.

Medicaid Policy: Medicaid Estate Recovery (in.gov) Open original page

What is a Miller trust?

A Miller trust (also called a Qualified Income Trust) lets a person whose income is over the Special Income Level still qualify for nursing home care or waiver services, by routing the extra income through the trust. Without a valid Miller trust, a person over that income level is not eligible.

Medicaid Policy: Miller Trust (in.gov); IHCPPM section 3010.20.15 Open original page

What is Indiana PathWays for Aging?

PathWays for Aging is Indiana's managed care program for Medicaid members who are 60 or older and eligible under an aged, blind, or disabled category, with or without Medicare. It covers long-term services, including nursing facility care and waiver services.

Indiana PathWays for Aging: Who is eligible for the PathWays program? (in.gov) Open original page

Where can I get help in a mental health crisis?

Call or text 988, or chat at 988Indiana.org, to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Trained crisis specialists answer 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, for thoughts of suicide, a mental health or substance use crisis, or any kind of emotional distress.

HIP

What is the Healthy Indiana Plan (HIP)?

HIP is Indiana's health coverage program for low-income Hoosier adults. Members have a POWER account and are covered under either HIP Plus or HIP Basic.

HIP: Frequently Asked Questions (in.gov) Open original page

What is the income limit for HIP?

The HIP Plus monthly income limit for a household of 2 is $2,489.20. For a household of 1 it is $1,835.50, and the limit goes up by $652.50 for each additional person. These are the 2026 limits, and a caseworker runs the full rules on your situation.

HIP: Federal Poverty Level Income Chart (in.gov) Open original page

What is a POWER account?

A POWER account is a special account that pays the first $2,500 of a member's covered medical costs each calendar year. The state pays most of the amount. The member pays a small monthly contribution, one of five amounts between $1 and $20 based on income.

HIP: Frequently Asked Questions (in.gov) Open original page

What is the difference between HIP Plus and HIP Basic?

HIP Plus is the plan members start on. It includes vision, dental, and chiropractic care, and members pay a monthly POWER account contribution instead of copayments (the one copay is $8 for using the emergency room when there is no emergency). HIP Basic is the fallback for members at or below 100 percent of the federal poverty level who do not make their contributions. Its benefits are reduced, vision, dental, and chiropractic are not covered, and copayments of $4 to $8 apply each time you get care.

HIP: Frequently Asked Questions (in.gov) Open original page

What are the HIP work requirements?

Beginning January 1, 2027, most HIP members ages 19 to 64 must spend at least 80 hours a month on work, school at least half time, an apprenticeship, a work program, or volunteering. Activities can be combined to reach 80 hours.

HIP: Information for HIP Members (in.gov) Open original page

Who does not have to meet the HIP work requirements?

Exemptions include caregivers of a person with a disability, parents or caregivers of a child age 13 or younger, people who are pregnant, former foster youth under age 26, members of federally recognized tribes, and veterans with a total (100 percent) disability rating.

HIP: Information for HIP Members (in.gov) Open original page

What happens if I do not pay my POWER account contribution?

Members with income at or below 100 percent of the federal poverty level who do not make their contributions are moved to HIP Basic, which has reduced benefits and copayments of $4 to $8 each time they receive care.

HIP: Frequently Asked Questions (in.gov) Open original page

SNAP and food help

What is SNAP?

SNAP (the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) provides monthly food benefits on an Electronic Benefits Transfer (EBT) card to low-income individuals and families. In Indiana, the Division of Family Resources (DFR) runs the program.

DFR: SNAP (Food Assistance), About SNAP (in.gov) Open original page

What are the SNAP income limits?

SNAP has two income tests: gross income, before deductions, and net income, after allowable deductions. For a household of 4, the monthly gross limit is $3,575 and the monthly net limit is $2,750. A caseworker runs the full rules on your situation.

DFR: SNAP Food Assistance, Income (in.gov) Open original page

How much can my household get from SNAP?

The maximum monthly benefit for a household of 4 is $1,023. A household of 1 can get up to $306, and each additional member raises the maximum by $225. Your actual benefit depends on your household's income.

DFR: SNAP Food Assistance, Income (in.gov) Open original page

How do I apply for SNAP?

Apply online through the FSSA Benefits Portal, at your local DFR office, or by phone at 1-800-403-0864.

DFR: SNAP (Food Assistance) (in.gov) Open original page

How fast can I get SNAP?

Eligible households must be able to get benefits within 30 days of applying. Households that qualify for expedited service get benefits within 7 days.

SNAP Application Processing Timeliness (USDA Food and Nutrition Service); DFR Policy Manual Chapter 2000, section 2020.05.05 Open original page

Can I apply for SNAP and cash assistance together?

Yes. One state application covers SNAP and cash assistance (TANF), and the FSSA Benefits Portal also takes health coverage applications.

Indiana application for SNAP and cash assistance (forms.in.gov) Open original page

How long do SNAP benefits stay on my EBT card?

Beginning November 23, 2026, SNAP benefits that are at least 274 days old (about nine months) will be removed from your EBT account, whether or not the card has been used. Benefits that are removed cannot be replaced.

DFR: SNAP (Food Assistance), About SNAP (in.gov) Open original page

Medicare

What is a Medicare Savings Program?

Medicare Savings Programs are state programs that help pay your Medicare Part A and Part B premiums. If you qualify, a program might also pay your Part A and Part B deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments. You apply through your state.

Medicare Savings Programs (Medicare.gov) Open original page

What does the QMB program pay?

The Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) program helps pay Part A premiums (if you do not get Part A premium-free), Part B premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments. For 2026, the individual monthly income limit is $1,350 and the resource limit is $9,950. A caseworker runs the full rules on your situation.

Medicare Savings Programs (Medicare.gov) Open original page

What is Extra Help for drug costs?

Extra Help is a Medicare program that helps people with limited income and resources pay Part D drug coverage costs, including premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance. You also pay no Part D late enrollment penalty while you have Extra Help. People with full Medicaid, a Medicare Savings Program, or SSI get Extra Help automatically.

Help with drug costs (Medicare.gov) Open original page

Can I have Medicare and Medicaid at the same time?

Yes. People who qualify for both are called dual eligible. In Indiana, PathWays Dual Care is a Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan that puts Medicare and Medicaid benefits in one plan, and FSSA manages the Medicaid part.

Indiana PathWays for Aging: Dual Eligible Special Needs Plan (in.gov) Open original page

Does Medicare pay for nursing home care?

Medicare does not cover custodial care, help with bathing, dressing, or eating, if that is the only care you need, and it generally does not cover long-term nursing home stays. Part A may cover short-term skilled nursing or rehabilitation care in a nursing home for up to 100 days.

Nursing Home Coverage (Medicare.gov) Open original page

How do I apply for a Medicare Savings Program in Indiana?

Apply through your state Medicaid agency. In Indiana, apply online through the FSSA Benefits Portal, in person at a local DFR office, by mail, or by phone at 1-800-403-0864. The state decides which Medicare Savings Program you qualify for.

Medicare Savings Programs (Medicare.gov); Indiana Medicaid for Members: Apply for Coverage (in.gov) Open original page

What if I disagree with a Medicare decision?

You can file an appeal. If you are in a Medicare Advantage plan or a drug plan, the plan must tell you in writing how to appeal. If you have Original Medicare, follow the instructions on the notice you received.

Disability and waivers

What is the income limit for a Medicaid waiver?

Waiver services use a Special Income Level of 300 percent of the SSI maximum benefit. Effective January 1, 2026, that limit is $2,982 a month. A caseworker runs the full rules on your situation.

IHCPPM section 3010.20.15 (Chapter 3000: Eligibility Standards) Open original page

Whose income counts for a waiver?

Only the applicant's own income counts toward the Special Income Level. There is no couple version of the limit, and a spouse's income is not counted for the applicant.

IHCPPM Chapter 3300: Medicaid Waivers Open original page

What is the asset limit for a Medicaid waiver?

The resource (asset) limit is $2,000 for a single person, and $3,000 for a married couple when both spouses apply. Resources are counted as of the first of the month.

IHCPPM Chapter 2600: Resources Open original page

What can my spouse keep if I am on a waiver?

Your spouse who stays at home (the community spouse) keeps a protected share of your combined assets: half of them, but not less than $32,532 and not more than $162,660 in 2026. The community spouse can also keep monthly income up to an allowance that ranges from $2,705 to $4,067. A caseworker confirms the exact amounts for your case.

IHCPPM Chapter 2600: Resources (community spouse rules) Open original page

Can my spouse be my paid caregiver?

Yes, but only through Structured Family Caregiving, where the caregiver is paid a per-diem through the agency. A spouse cannot be the paid caregiver under Attendant Care, Home and Community Assistance, Respite, or Participant-Directed Home Care services.

DDARS: Medicaid HCBS Waivers (in.gov); IHCPPM Chapter 3300: Medicaid Waivers Open original page

Does Structured Family Caregiving pay count as income for Medicaid?

No. Income from the Structured Family Caregiving program is exempt for all Medicaid categories and does not count in the Medicaid budget. The waiver provider sends the caregiving agreement to DFR so the income is coded correctly.

IHCPPM section 2810.55.00 (Chapter 2800: Income) Open original page

What happens if I give away assets before applying for a waiver?

Giving away assets for less than fair value is reviewed over a 60-month look-back period. A transfer in that window can cause a penalty period, during which Medicaid will not pay for long-term care.

IHCPPM Chapter 2600: Resources Open original page

Does my home count against the waiver asset limit?

Your home is treated under its own rules, but Indiana applies a home equity limit of $752,000 for long-term care and waiver eligibility.

IHCPPM Chapter 2600: Resources Open original page

What Medicaid waivers does Indiana offer?

Indiana's home and community-based waivers include the Health and Wellness Waiver (formerly the Aged and Disabled Waiver) for people age 59 and younger with a disability who would otherwise need nursing facility care, and the Community Integration and Habilitation Waiver and Family Supports Waiver for children and adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Adults 60 and older receive long-term services through PathWays for Aging.

DDARS: Medicaid HCBS Waivers (in.gov) Open original page

How can I get help finding a job if I have a disability?

Vocational Rehabilitation (VR) helps people with disabilities prepare for, get, keep, or advance in a job. Start with an online referral; your local VR office will then contact you to schedule an intake appointment.

DDARS: Vocational Rehabilitation Referral (in.gov) Open original page

Social Security

What is the Ticket to Work program?

Ticket to Work is a free and voluntary Social Security program for people ages 18 through 64 who receive SSDI or SSI and want to work. It connects you with service providers that give the supports and services you need to find and keep employment.

What is Social Security's Ticket to Work Program? (Choose Work, Social Security) Open original page

What is a Trial Work Period?

If you receive SSDI, the Trial Work Period lets you test your ability to work for at least 9 months while you keep your full SSDI benefits, no matter how much you earn, as long as you report your work activity. The 9 months do not have to be in a row; they are counted within a rolling 60-month period. In 2026, a month counts as a service month if you earn $1,210 or more.

Trial Work Period (Choose Work, Social Security) Open original page

What is the SSI resource limit?

For SSI, the limit for countable resources is $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple. Some resources do not count, including the home you live in and one vehicle used for transportation.

SSI Resources (Social Security) Open original page

What if Social Security says it overpaid me?

An overpayment means you received more money than you should have for one or more months. Social Security will mail you a notice explaining the amount and your options. You can appeal the decision, ask for a waiver of the overpayment, or repay it. Contact Social Security within 60 days; if you do not, they may begin reducing your monthly benefits to recover the money.

Overpayments: What You Need to Know (Choose Work, Social Security) Open original page

How do I appeal a Social Security decision?

You can appeal most decisions Social Security makes about whether you can get SSI, or about changes to your benefit amount. The first level of appeal is reconsideration, a fresh review of your case. After that, you can request a hearing with an administrative law judge.

Understanding SSI: Appeals Process (Social Security) Open original page

Child care and families

How can I get help paying for child care?

Indiana's Child Care Development Fund (CCDF) provides vouchers to help low-income families pay for child care. Families earning less than 135 percent of the federal poverty level can qualify if they need care because of employment or an education or training program.

OECOSL: Child Care Vouchers (in.gov) Open original page

Who can get a child care voucher, and is there a waitlist?

The child must be under age 13, or over 13 with documented special needs up to the 19th birthday, and must be a U.S. citizen or qualified legal alien. Your family must live in the county where you apply and choose a CCDF-eligible provider. A waitlist for new applicants has been in place since 2024; it applies to new applicants and new children added to a voucher, and current voucher holders are not affected.

OECOSL: Child Care Vouchers (in.gov) Open original page

What is On My Way Pre-K?

On My Way Pre-K is Indiana's state-funded program that provides free, high-quality pre-K to 4-year-old children from families with income at or below 135 percent of the federal poverty level. Children learn in approved public schools, private centers, ministries, and licensed homes.

OECOSL: On My Way Pre-K (in.gov) Open original page

What is TANF?

TANF (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families) provides cash assistance and supportive services to families with children under age 18, to help them reach economic self-sufficiency. The Division of Family Resources decides eligibility.

DFR: About TANF (in.gov) Open original page

What is First Steps?

First Steps is Indiana's early intervention program for infants and toddlers, from birth to the third birthday, who are experiencing developmental delays or have a diagnosed condition likely to result in a delay. It connects families with services that support their child's development. Contact the First Steps office for your county to start.
